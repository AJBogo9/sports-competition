# Community mobilisation briefing: activating nine guilds through a text-only bot

Compiled 2026-09-08 for the "active days per member" proposal. Every citation was checked against
Crossref, Europe PMC or the publisher's page; figures are quoted from the abstract or full text
where it could be retrieved, and the entry says where it could not. Grades: A meta-analysis or
replicated RCT; B single RCT or field experiment; C observational, secondary analysis or case
study; D self-report, anecdote or untested theory. Sources already in docs/evidence.md are cited by
section number.

## 1. Summary

1. Identification moves effort only when the group identity is salient and good performance is seen as in the group's interest (van Knippenberg 2000, C). Guild rank supplies the second; the guild chat supplies the first.
2. Intergroup competition removes the free riding that a collective reward causes (Erev et al. 1993, B: a 30% production loss under team pay, eliminated by team-versus-team), and works best between evenly matched teams.
3. A team-only reward with no individual component did nothing (Patel et al. 2016, B). The personal target, streak and celebration are load-bearing, not decoration.
4. Health behaviour spreads when a person sees several people they know adopt it (Centola 2010, B: 53.77% adoption in clustered networks versus 38.26% in random ones) and reaches the least active through similar others (Centola 2011, B). The active are moved by the less active, not the reverse (Aral and Nicolaides 2017, C).
5. Seeding through nominated friends beats seeding through the most connected people (Kim et al. 2015, B: +12.2% versus random; most-connected no better than random). "Bring one guildmate" is the text-only form.
6. Broadcast averages pull the top down (Chen et al. 2010, B: above-median users cut contributions by 62%). "Active days per member" must be the ranking rule, never the displayed number.
7. Dynamic norms are real but very small (2026 meta-analysis, 79 studies, A: r = .03 to .10). Use one when the count rose, never when it fell.
8. Team effects in statewide challenges are modest (ICC about 0.10) and come from what teammates do, not from encouragement features; self-selected captains who recruited their team did better themselves (Leahey et al. 2012, C).
9. Opinion-leader programmes work when leaders are community-identified and talk often (Kelly 1991, B) and fail when those elements are dropped (NIMH 2010, B). The bot cannot do this; the organiser can, once per guild.
10. The proposal is sound as a scoring rule. Its risks are in the display and in the individual layer, both fixable in copy.

## 2. Findings by question

### Q1. Social identity and group motivation

**Ellemers, de Gilder and Haslam 2004** (D, theory). A collectively shared identity raises motivation in groups, and leaders are effective to the degree they create and communicate that identity. No numbers; the publisher page refused fetching and the summary is from the VU Amsterdam and AMR records. **van Knippenberg 2000** (C, review) gives the conditional that matters: identification predicts motivation and performance "to the extent that (a) social identity is salient, and (b) high performance is perceived to be in the group's or organisation's interest". **Riketta 2005** (A, k = 96) confirms identification correlates with in-role and extra-role behaviour; the coefficients sit behind a paywall and are not quoted here. **Worchel et al. 1998** (B, three lab experiments): making the group identifiable and important raised individual productivity in group settings.

**Erev, Bornstein and Galili 1993** (B, field experiment, groups of four picking oranges). "The collective reward rule resulted in a 30% loss in production compared to the personal payoff rule. The intergroup competition eliminated this loss of productivity and was more effective the more similar the competing teams were." **Puurtinen and Mappes 2009** (B, lab public goods): between-group competition "increases within-group cooperation and overall productivity" and "intensifies the moral emotions of anger and guilt associated with violations of the cooperative norm". The second clause is a warning: the mechanism that raises effort also raises resentment of non-contributors, who in a 500-member guild are the silent majority.

**Steffens et al. 2020** (A, k = 128, N = 32,834). A leader's group prototypicality has a moderate-to-large effect, stronger when followers identify with the group. **Reicher, Haslam and Hopkins 2005** (D) call this leaders as "entrepreneurs of identity". Messages that ask a guild to act should come in the guild's voice or from a guild member, not from a central organiser. **Karau and Williams 1993** (evidence.md 5.8, A): loafing grows with group size unless contributions are identifiable.

What a competition between pre-existing guilds can lean on: salience is free, the rank makes logging a group interest, and per-member normalisation keeps the nine evenly matched, which Erev et al. found made intergroup competition most effective. What it cannot lean on: identity producing effort from someone who does not believe their own day changes the outcome.

### Q2. Behaviour spread in networks

**Centola 2010** (B, 1,528 participants). "On average, the behavior reached 53.77% of the clustered networks, whereas only 38.26% of the population adopted in the random networks"; diffusion was "more than four times faster", and participants "were significantly more likely to adopt after receiving a second signal than after receiving only one signal (P < 0.001)". **Centola 2011** (B): "homophily significantly increased overall adoption of a new health behavior, especially among those most in need of it". **Aral and Nicolaides 2017** (C, 1.1 million runners, weather as instrument): "an additional kilometre run by friends influences ego to run an additional 3/10th of a kilometre"; "less active runners influence more active runners, while the reverse is not true"; men influence men, women influence both, men do not influence women. Complex contagion did not explain their data, so Centola's multiple-signal rule is a heuristic, not a law.

**Christakis and Fowler 2007** (C) and the critiques **Shalizi and Thomas 2011** and **Lyons 2011**: homophily, contagion and shared environment are "confounded with each other" in observational networks. Only experiments carry causal weight here.

**Kim et al. 2015** (B, cluster RCT, 32 villages, 5,773 people). Seeding nominated friends of random villagers raised uptake by 12.2% over random (95% CI 6.9 to 17.9); seeding the most connected "produced no greater adoption ... compared with random targeting". **Airoldi and Christakis 2024** (B, 176 villages, 24,702 people) replicated it over 22 months: "knowledge spread more readily than behavior", spillovers reached two degrees, and "outcomes that were intrinsically easier to adopt also manifested greater spillovers". **Paluck, Shepherd and Aronow 2016** (B, 56 schools, 24,191 students). Seed groups of 20 to 32 students took a public stance; conflict fell 25% in the original abstract and 30% in the twice-corrected text, more when seeds were "social referents". The seeds ran a grassroots poster-and-wristband campaign with no persuasive unit.

For quiet members of a large existing group: they start when several people in their own cluster visibly do it and when those people resemble them. One broadcast is one signal. The cheapest supported lever is asking each registrant to bring one guildmate: it recruits through friends, into clusters, and by the friendship paradox into better-connected people than random outreach reaches.

### Q3. Collective action, critical mass and norms

**Oliver, Marwell and Teixeira 1985; Marwell and Oliver 1993** (D, formal theory). Collective goods are typically provided by a small, highly interested subset, the critical mass, whose early contributions raise the value of later ones under an accelerating production function. **Granovetter 1978** (D): a visible count of adopters trips the low-threshold members first. The active minority in each guild is the critical mass; the proposal's redirection of them into recruiting is what the theory says a critical mass is for.

**Croson and Marks 2000** (A within lab experiments): in threshold public goods "higher SRs [step returns] lead to more contributions". Make the return of one more day visible ("34 active days behind FK"), not the abstract share. **Cox, Arnold and Villamayor-Tomás 2010** (C, 91 studies): Ostrom's principles are "well supported empirically". Transferable: clear membership boundaries (rosters), monitoring visible to members (the pin), rules fitted to local conditions. Not transferable: sanctions and conflict resolution; a bot must not police. **Balliet 2010** (A, 45 effect sizes): communication raises cooperation, d = 1.01, face-to-face d = 1.21 versus written d = 0.46, "stronger in larger ... group social dilemmas". This does not license chat features; Zhang 2016 (evidence.md 1.1) tested a chat among strangers and it fell below control. The guilds already have chats; the bot posts into them.

**Frey and Meier 2004** (B, field experiment, Zurich). Told 64% of students had contributed, 77.0% contributed; told 46%, 74.7%; control 72.9%. The high message helped significantly; the low one did not differ from control. **Chen et al. 2010** (B, MovieLens): after seeing the median, "users below the median demonstrate a 530 percent increase ... while those above the median decrease their ratings by 62 percent". **Shang and Croson 2009** (B): the most influential social information was a figure at the 90th to 95th percentile of prior gifts, +12%, among new members only. With **Schultz et al. 2007** (evidence.md 5.2): a broadcast average is a target the people above it fall toward; a high exemplar pulls up; the effect lands on newcomers.

**Sparkman and Walton 2017** (B). Both café messages used the same minority figure; static: "30% of Americans make an effort to limit their meat consumption"; dynamic: "in the last 5 years, 30% of Americans have now started to make an effort". Meatless orders doubled, 34% versus 17%. **Replication is mixed**: Aldoh, Sparks and Harris 2021 and 2024 (N = 1,294) "found no positive effect of dynamic norms (versus static norms) on any outcome". **Reintgen Kamphuisen, Bouman and van der Werff 2026** (A, 79 studies): effects "very small (r = .03 to .10, across different analytical approaches)", larger with general reference groups, short timeframes, people as the subject of change, and descriptive plus injunctive appeals combined. A dynamic norm is a cheap line, not a strategy.

On count versus share early on: no trial isolates the two. The mechanism evidence is one-sided. A share of a 500-member roster at the base rate SPEC.md expects is a low norm and a target the active fall toward (Schultz, Chen). A count carries no denominator, cannot be read as "most people don't", and supports the dynamic frame when it rises. Frey and Meier's low message did not fall below control, so the downside is bounded, but nothing is gained by taking it.

### Q4. Team spirit in health competitions

**Zhang et al. 2016** (evidence.md 1.1, B). Six-person teams with team incentives and a chat tool: 16.8 attendances per week against 20.3 for control; support "grew at a significantly slower rate than in the control condition" (p < 0.001); the chat produced 81 messages in the whole programme. **Patel et al. 2019** (evidence.md 5.7, B): competition in groups of three was the only social arm that persisted; collaboration was weakest and not sustained.

**Patel et al. 2016** (B, 304 employees in 76 four-person teams). Share of participant-days meeting a 7,000-step goal: combined individual-plus-team incentive 0.35 versus control 0.18 (p < 0.001); individual alone 0.25 (n.s.); team-only 0.17, "difference: -0.003". **Kullgren et al. 2013** (B, 105 employees): a group incentive beat an individual one for weight loss (4.4 kg versus control; 3.2 kg versus individual), but the pot was split only among members who met their own goal. Pattern: individual goal plus group consequence works; group consequence alone does not.

**Leahey et al. 2010** (C, Shape Up Rhode Island 2007, 5,333 adults in 652 teams of 7 to 10). Steps rose from 7,029 to 9,393 per day; team ICC 0.09; "being on a more active team was associated with greater increases ... whereas being on a team with a broad range of steps was associated with smaller changes". **Leahey et al. 2012** (C, SURI 2009, 3,330 completers in 987 teams of 9.0 ± 2.1). Team ICC 0.10; share of teammates in the same division and self-reported teammate influence predicted outcomes; team size did not (p = 0.35). Teams "were formed by self-selected captains who recruited team members ... Team captains received no formal training nor incentive", and captains lost more (3.7% versus 3.4%, p = 0.02). **Wing et al. 2009** (C): of 4,717 enrolled, 3,311 completed 12 weeks. **Ganesan et al. 2016** (C, uncontrolled, 69,219 in worksite teams): +3,519 steps per day, no control. **Wang et al. 2023** (evidence.md 1.4, B): +105.8 steps per day, decaying weekly.

Reading across: team spirit *features* never beat a comparison arm and once fell below control. Team *membership* effects exist, are modest, and are driven by what teammates actually do. The recruiter is the one team-spirit element with a positive signal, and it benefits the recruiter. Team size within 5 to 11 did not matter; nothing approaches 350 to 700, the gap evidence.md §4 already flags.

### Q5. Mobilisation from organising and public health

**Kelly et al. 1991** (B, one intervention city, two comparison). Opinion leaders "reliably identified" within the community were trained and "complied in talking frequently with friends and acquaintances"; unprotected anal intercourse fell from 36.9% to 27.5% with "little or no change" elsewhere. **NIMH Collaborative Trial 2010** (B, five countries): "both intervention and comparison conditions showed declines of approximately 33%" with no difference, attributed to intensive services in both arms. **Kelly 2004** (C): the UK programmes that failed "did not incorporate many of POL's core or essential elements".

**Ganz 2010** (D, practitioner theory) and **Han 2014** (C, comparative cases): organising develops members' capacity to lead others through relationships, a shared story (self, us, now), strategy and action; mobilising issues transactional asks; effective associations do both. **Rogers 2003** (D): adoption depends on relative advantage, compatibility, low complexity, trialability and observability.

Borrow: the sociometric step (ask each guild board who two or three well-liked, ordinary-fitness members are, and ask them to log visibly in weeks one and two); the friendship-nomination ask at registration; observability (the pin) and trialability (one tap). Do not borrow: trained peer educators, structured conversations, any role the bot would have to police. The POL trials show the mechanism is human contact at frequency; the bot supplies the observable behaviour those contacts point at.

## 3. Assessment of "active days per member"

**Does it support the mechanisms?**

- *Identity.* Yes. It meets van Knippenberg's condition (b): more members logging is unambiguously the guild's interest, and per-member normalisation keeps the nine evenly matched, which Erev et al. found made intergroup competition most effective.
- *Contagion.* Indirectly. The rule rewards breadth, but a rule is invisible; contagion runs on what members see. It helps only if the display makes the count of active people visible in each guild's cluster.
- *Critical mass.* Yes, its strongest fit. The active minority's extra minutes become worthless to the score, so their only marginal action is recruiting. Aral and Nicolaides add a twist: the active are moved by the less active, so a recruit's first log should be as visible to the recruiter as the reverse.
- *Dynamic norms.* Compatible, provided the displayed number is a count that can rise week to week.

**Where it is exposed.**

1. *A per-member average is a share in disguise.* "1.4 active days per member" reads as "the average guildmate is active 1.4 days a week": a low descriptive norm broadcast to everyone above it. Chen et al. is the closest analogue and the above-median group cut contributions by 62%. This is the one point where the proposal conflicts with the project's own no-share rule.
2. *No individual consequence.* Patel et al. 2016 is direct evidence that a team-only reward does nothing. Under the cap a 60-minute day and a 15-minute day are identical to the guild; that is safe only because the personal target, streak and celebration remain. The guild score must not become the only feedback.
3. *Untested at this scale.* Every positive team result is from groups of three to eleven.

**Tweaks to the scoring rule.** Keep it, with two clarifications: only a tier of 15 minutes or more is an active day, a "rest" log counts for nothing; and divide by the full roster, not registrants, or registering becomes a cost. Leave the seven-day ceiling; the WHO plateau is about volume, and a 15-minute walk on a rest day is not the harm a daily streak would be. No other change: sub-squads, new-logger bonuses and tier weighting each reintroduce a share or a duration incentive.

**Tweaks to what is communicated**, all within a text-only bot and none needing kudos, chat, badges or prizes:

- *The pin.* Rank, the count of people active this week in this guild, and the gap to the adjacent guild in active days ("34 active days behind FK", "12 ahead of PT"). Never the per-member average. The gap in days is Croson and Marks's step return made visible; the count is the observable adoption signal Centola and Rogers point at.
- *The Monday post.* Winner, the reader's guild's gap in active days, the count of people who logged (already the rule), and one dynamic line only when the count rose ("more of us logged than last week"), with an injunctive half ("and that is what moves the table"). Never a falling count.
- *Registration and first confirmation.* One sentence in the guild's voice explaining the rule as a group interest ("your day counts exactly as much as anyone's; the guild climbs by more people, not longer runs"), and one nomination ask: "Who in your guild would do this? Send them the link." That is Kim et al.'s friendship-nomination targeting at zero cost and no extra tap on the log.
- *The organiser, outside the bot.* Before launch, ask each guild board to name two or three well-liked, ordinary-fitness members and ask them to log and say so in the guild chat in weeks one and two: Kelly's sociometric step and Paluck's seed group in miniature, human, once, no code.

## 4. What the literature says should not be done

- No share of the roster or per-member average displayed anywhere (Schultz 2007; Chen 2010). Ranks and counts only.
- No chat, encouragement, kudos or collaboration mechanics (Zhang 2016; Patel 2019). Communication matters (Balliet 2010), but the guild already has a chat.
- Never let the guild score be a member's only feedback (Patel 2016).
- Do not seed through the most connected or the fittest (Kim 2015; Centola 2011).
- Never send a falling dynamic norm, and do not expect a rising one to carry the campaign (r = .03 to .10).
- Do not speak for the guild in the organiser's voice (Steffens 2020; Reicher 2005).
- Do not point at non-contributors, even implicitly ("most people forget"); intergroup competition already raises anger at norm violators (Puurtinen and Mappes 2009).
- Do not cite observational contagion as proof logging will spread (Shalizi and Thomas 2011); the experiments show behaviour spreads less readily than knowledge (Airoldi and Christakis 2024).
- Do not put a critical-mass target in copy ("we need 100 people"); a missed threshold is a broadcast failure and the framing is untested here.
